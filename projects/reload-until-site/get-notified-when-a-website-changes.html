How to get notified when a website changes

Restocks, exam results, appointment slots, status pages: pick the method that fits the page, not the other way round.

"Tell me when this page changes" covers very different situations. A status page that offers email alerts needs no tool. A booking page behind a login can only be watched from your own browser. Fifty product pages over a month call for a background monitor. Here's how to tell which case you're in.

Step 1: check whether the site already notifies you

This is the most reliable option, and people often skip it:

  • Status pages (the "status.something.com" kind) usually have a Subscribe button for email, RSS or webhooks.
  • Shops often have a "notify me when available" button on sold-out products.
  • Exam boards and government portals sometimes send SMS or email when results or documents are ready. Look for it in your account settings.
  • Blogs, forums and release pages often have an RSS or Atom feed. Try adding /feed or .rss to the address, or check the page source for "rss".

If one of these exists, use it. The rest of this page is for when none does.

Watching a tab

Open the page and let a tool reload it, check for a specific text, and alert you. You can do this with a short userscript or a few lines of Python (see the three methods in this guide), or with an extension such as Reload Until, which I make. It reloads on your interval, stops when your text appears or disappears, and alerts you with a notification and a sound.

Background monitors

Services and extensions such as Distill Web Monitor and Visualping check pages on a schedule and alert you by email or push when something changes. They're the right choice for watching many pages over a long time. Check their free-tier limits and how often they check.

Step 3: watch for the right thing

  • Watch a specific text, not "any change". Pages change constantly (ads, timestamps, "people viewing now"), and "any change" alerts turn into noise. "Add to cart" appearing or "No appointments available" disappearing is a much better signal.
  • Watch for the bad text to disappear when the good state has no fixed wording. Waiting for "Sold out" to vanish catches every possible "in stock" label.
  • Limit the check to one part of the page when the same word also appears elsewhere, for example in a menu or a footer.

Step 4: be a good citizen

  • Check only as often as you need to. For most pages, once a minute is plenty.
  • Read the site's terms. Some booking and ticketing sites forbid automated access or lock accounts that reload too often. Their rules apply whatever tool you use.
  • Monitoring tools only watch. Don't use anything that books, buys or submits forms for you where the site doesn't allow it.
